## 1. Seven CV mistakes that cost people interviews in Ghana

*Best on: LinkedIn article, Medium*

Many people who do not get called for interviews are not short of ability. Their CV lets them down in small ways that are easy to fix. Here are seven common ones.

**1. One CV for every job.** A recruiter wants to see that you fit their job. Change your opening lines and the order of your points to match each advert.

**2. Long blocks of writing.** A recruiter reads on a phone or between meetings. Short lines that start with a verb, such as "Prepared monthly stock reports", are quicker to read than a paragraph.

**3. Duties with no result.** "Responsible for customers" tells me where you sat. "Handled customer complaints at the front desk and followed each one to a close" tells me what you did. Add a number only if it is true.

**4. Spelling and grammar slips.** One slip can make a careful person look careless. Read your CV aloud, then ask a friend to read it.

**5. An email address that does not look professional.** Make a simple one with your name.

**6. Gaps nobody explains.** If you were out of work, studying or caring for family, say so in one short, honest line.

**7. Claims you could not explain in an interview.** If a line says you "managed" something and you only helped with it, change it. Interviewers ask about every claim, and an honest CV is easier to defend.

A good CV is not a fancy one. It is a clear, true one that is written for the job in front of you.

## 2. How to write a cover letter for a job in Ghana, step by step

*Best on: Medium, LinkedIn article*

A cover letter, often called a letter of application, is your first chance to speak to the employer in your own words. In Ghana it follows a formal layout. Get the layout right and the reader sees that you take the job seriously.

**Step 1: Your details, top right.** Put your name, your town or city, your email and your phone number on the right-hand side.

**Step 2: The date.** Write it under your details.

**Step 3: The employer, on the left.** Write "The Human Resource Manager" or the person's name and position, and the organisation.

**Step 4: The greeting.** Use "Dear Sir/Madam," if you do not know a name. If you do know it, write "Dear Mr Mensah," or "Dear Madam Owusu,".

**Step 5: A bold subject line.** Name the job in capital letters, for example: APPLICATION FOR THE POSITION OF ACCOUNTS OFFICER.

**Step 6: Three short paragraphs.** First, say which job you want and where you saw it. Second, give two or three reasons you fit, using real examples from work or school. Third, say your CV is attached and you would welcome an interview.

**Step 7: The closing.** Write "Yours faithfully," if you began with "Dear Sir/Madam". Write "Yours sincerely," if you began with a name. Then type your full name.

Keep it to one page. Use the employer's name and the exact job title from the advert. Never copy a letter word for word from the internet, because employers notice.

## 3. You finished national service. Here is how to write your first CV.

*Best on: Facebook groups for graduates, LinkedIn*

Congratulations on finishing national service. Many graduates say, "I have no experience." That is not true. You have a year of work, and it belongs on your CV.

**List it like a job.** Write your role, the place where you served, your dates, and two to four lines on what you did. For example: "Teaching Assistant, a senior high school, October to September. Taught Core Mathematics to Form 1 and marked class assessments."

**Put your education near the top.** Your degree or diploma, the institution and the years. Employers know you are new, so they look there first.

**Add your projects and your dissertation** if they connect to the job. One or two lines are enough.

**Show leadership.** Did you lead a club, run an event or help in your church or community? Add it, briefly.

**List real skills.** Microsoft Excel, writing, a language you speak, a system you have used. Only list what you can do.

**Keep it to one or two pages.** A short, clean CV beats a long one.

**Do not exaggerate.** A clear, true line about helping with a task is stronger than a large claim that falls apart at the interview.

## 4. What is an ATS, and does your CV need to pass one?

*Best on: Medium, Quora*

If you apply to a bigger employer or through an online platform, your CV may first go into an applicant-tracking system, called an ATS for short. It is software that receives CVs, reads their text and files them so recruiters can search.

**What it reads.** Text. It does not see pictures or fancy design. If your CV is a photo of a page, there is nothing for it to read.

**What helps it.**
- Real, selectable text. Save your CV as a PDF from a word processor, not a scan.
- Standard headings such as Experience, Education and Skills.
- A simple layout. Heavy tables, text boxes and graphics can scramble the order.
- Your contact details in the body of the page.
- The words from the job advert, used where they truly describe you.

**What does not matter as much as people say.** Nobody can promise that a certain format "beats" every system, and many smaller employers read CVs directly. A clean, simple CV works well either way.

**Remember the person at the end.** Even if software sorts your CV, a person reads it next. It must be clear, honest and specific.

## 5. How to turn one CV into five, without starting again

*Best on: LinkedIn article*

If you apply for several jobs, you do not need five different CVs. You need one strong base CV, and a few quick changes for each job.

**1. Keep a master CV.** Put every job, qualification and skill in it. This is your source. You will never send it as it is.

**2. Read the advert twice.** Underline the duties, the skills and the qualifications it asks for. What it mentions first, or twice, matters most.

**3. Rewrite your opening lines.** Use the job title and the two or three skills the advert stresses. Keep every word true.

**4. Move the best evidence up.** Under each job, put the points that match the advert first. Shorten what does not matter for this role.

**5. Reorder your skills.** The skills the advert names come first.

**6. Check each line.** Can you explain it in an interview? If not, change it.

**7. Match your cover letter.** Use the same job title and one or two of the same examples.

This takes about twenty or thirty minutes by hand for each job. It is worth it, because a CV that looks made for the job gets noticed.

## 6. How to write the opening lines of your CV (with four examples)

*Best on: Quora, Medium*

The profile, or summary, is the first thing a recruiter reads, so it has to work. In three or four lines, say who you are, what you do and what you offer.

**A simple method:**
1. Who you are: your job title or qualification.
2. Your experience: how long, and in what area.
3. Two things you do well that the job needs.
4. What you are looking for, in a short phrase.

**Four examples (made-up people, so use your own true details):**

*Accounts assistant:* "Accounts assistant with three years of experience in payables, reconciliations and month-end reporting. Careful with detail and comfortable with Excel. Looking for an accounts role where accuracy and reliability matter."

*Registered nurse:* "Registered General Nurse with five years of experience on a medical ward, giving medication, monitoring patients and working closely with doctors."

*Teacher:* "Trained teacher with four years of experience teaching Integrated Science at junior high level. Plans lessons clearly, keeps accurate records and supports pupils who need extra help."

*Fresh graduate:* "Recent BSc Marketing graduate who has completed national service in a customer-facing role. Strong written communication and social media skills, and keen to start a career in marketing."

**Avoid** empty words such as "hardworking team player" with nothing to back them up, and anything you could not explain in an interview.

## 7. What a hospital wants to see on a nurse's CV

*Best on: Facebook groups for nurses, LinkedIn*

If you are a nurse or midwife, the person reading your CV wants to know three things quickly: are you registered, where have you worked, and what can you do safely.

**Put these first.**
- Your name, town and phone number.
- A short profile: your registration, your years of experience and the setting you know, such as a medical ward.
- Your registration and licence details, if you hold them. Check that the number is correct.

**Describe real duties.** For each post, give the hospital, the ward or unit and your dates. Then write short lines: giving medication, monitoring vital signs, taking part in ward rounds, mentoring students, keeping accurate records.

**List your training.** Your nursing or midwifery qualification, and short courses such as Basic Life Support with the year.

**Include placements and national service** if they were in a clinical setting.

**Keep it to two pages.** A newly qualified nurse can often fit on one.

Do not invent numbers. If you do know a true figure, such as the size of your ward, it can help.

## 8. A CV for teachers: what a school wants to see

*Best on: Facebook groups for teachers, LinkedIn*

A school wants to know what you teach, at what level, and how well you run a class. Your CV can show that in plain words.

**Start with a short profile.** Your subjects, the level you teach and your years of experience.

**Show your teaching qualification** and, if you hold one, your licence or registration details.

**Under each school,** write the school, your dates, the classes and subjects you taught, and the work beyond the classroom: planning lessons, setting and marking assessments, keeping records, supporting pupils who need help, and clubs or sports you ran.

**Add your training:** workshops and in-service courses.

**Choose referees carefully.** A head teacher or lecturer who knows your work is a good choice. Ask first, and give their position and phone number.

**If you did national service as a teacher,** list it as experience.

Keep it to two pages, and keep every line true.

## 9. CV or resume: which one should you send?

*Best on: Quora*

People use "CV" and "resume" as if they were the same, and in daily use they often are. The words differ a little from country to country.

**In Ghana,** employers normally say "CV" for the document you send with an application. It is usually a two-page summary of your work, education and skills.

**In the United States and Canada,** a "resume" is the short document for most jobs, and a "CV" is a longer one for academic, research and medical posts.

**In the United Kingdom and much of Africa,** "CV" is the usual word for the standard job document.

**What to send:** follow the advert. If it asks for a CV, send a tidy two-page CV. If it asks for a resume, send the same kind of document, kept to one or two pages. For lectureships, research posts and scholarships, send an academic CV that lists publications and teaching.

A cover letter is not part of the CV. It is a separate letter that goes with it.

## 10. Why an honest CV wins more interviews

*Best on: LinkedIn article*

It is tempting to make your CV sound bigger than your real job. You may have "helped" with a task and want to write "led" it. Or you add a number to make a point.

Here is why that costs you.

**Interviewers test every line.** They ask "tell me more about that" because they want to see if you can. If you cannot explain a claim, the whole CV stops being trusted.

**A true CV is easier to defend.** When every line is real, you can answer calmly, with details. That confidence is what an interviewer remembers.

**Small honest lines carry weight.** "Assisted with monthly stock reports" is a fair claim. It shows you know what you did and what you did not do.

**You can still sound strong.** Honest does not mean weak. Describe real duties clearly, in short lines that start with a verb. Say what the work led to, when you know it. Show your training and your growth.

**The rule is simple:** if you could not explain it in an interview, take it out. A CV you can stand behind is worth more than one that looks bigger on paper.
